Unit Four

Genetics

How traits move from parents to offspring — from the molecule (DNA) to the pattern (Mendel's laws) to the real-world application (disorders, testing, and breeding).

4.1

The genetic material

+

Genetics is the branch of biology that studies how genetic material is organized and how traits pass from one generation to the next through genes. In almost every organism the genetic material is DNA. A handful of viruses — including HIV and the coronavirus that causes COVID-19 — use RNA instead.

Exam angleThis single fact ("DNA is the genetic material in nearly all organisms, RNA in some viruses") is a common true/false or fill-in-the-blank item.
4.2

Structure & function of DNA and RNA

+

DNA structure

DNA is a double helix — two strands twisted around each other like a spiral ladder. The sugar (deoxyribose) and phosphate groups form the two side-rails, and pairs of nitrogenous bases form the rungs.

The basic building block is the nucleotide: one sugar + one phosphate + one nitrogenous base. There are four bases, split into two chemical families:

FamilyBasesPairs with
PurinesAdenine (A), Guanine (G)A↔T, G↔C
PyrimidinesThymine (T), Cytosine (C)T↔A, C↔G

Each strand has a direction — a 5′ end and a 3′ end — and the two strands run antiparallel (opposite directions), coiled into a right-handed spiral. James Watson and Francis Crick discovered this double-helix structure.

Double-helix backbone with base-pair "rungs" — not to scale

Genes & chromosomes

A gene is a section of DNA that carries the instructions for one characteristic. A chromosome is a thread-like structure made of DNA wound around a protein called histone. A single chromosome can carry hundreds to thousands of genes, each at its own fixed position called a locus (plural: loci).

Chromosomes exist in pairs in diploid organisms — one inherited from the mother, one from the father. Humans have 46 chromosomes, arranged in 23 pairs.

DNA replication

Before a cell divides, its DNA must be copied so each new cell gets a full set. DNA replication is semi-conservative: each new double helix contains one original (parent) strand and one newly made strand.

RNA

RNA differs from DNA in three key ways: it is usually single-stranded, its sugar is ribose (not deoxyribose), and it uses the base Uracil (U) in place of Thymine. RNA's main job is carrying out the instructions in DNA — especially during protein synthesis (see 4.4).

Likely exam question"List three structural differences between DNA and RNA." → strand number, sugar type, and U vs T.
4.3

Cell division

+

Cells divide for two very different purposes — growth/repair, and sexual reproduction — and use two different processes accordingly.

MitosisMeiosis
PurposeGrowth, repair, asexual reproductionFormation of gametes (sperm/egg) for sexual reproduction
DivisionsOneTwo (Meiosis I & II)
Daughter cells2, diploid (2n)4, haploid (n)
Genetic identityIdentical to parent cellGenetically unique (crossing over + independent assortment)

Phases of mitosis

  1. Prophase — chromosomes condense and become visible; the mitotic spindle begins forming.
  2. Metaphase — chromosomes line up along the cell's equator (the metaphase plate).
  3. Anaphase — sister chromatids are pulled apart toward opposite poles.
  4. Telophase & cytokinesis — two new nuclei form and the cytoplasm divides, producing two identical daughter cells.

Meiosis — the source of genetic variation

Meiosis includes everything mitosis does, but twice, with one crucial extra event: in Prophase I, homologous chromosomes pair up and exchange segments — a process called crossing over — and in Metaphase I, homologous pairs line up and separate randomly (the physical basis of independent assortment, see 4.5). Together these two events are why siblings (other than identical twins) are never genetically identical.

4.4

Protein synthesis

+

Genes don't build the body directly — they code for proteins, which then carry out almost every job in the cell. This flow of information is sometimes called the central dogma: DNA → RNA → Protein.

  1. Transcription (in the nucleus) — an enzyme (RNA polymerase) copies one strand of a gene into a complementary strand of messenger RNA (mRNA).
  2. mRNA processing & export — the mRNA travels out of the nucleus to a ribosome.
  3. Translation (at the ribosome) — the ribosome reads the mRNA three bases (a codon) at a time. Transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules, each carrying a matching anticodon and a specific amino acid, bring the correct amino acids in sequence.
  4. Polypeptide formation — amino acids are joined into a chain, which folds into a functional protein.
4.5

Mendelian inheritance

+

Gregor Mendel, an Austrian monk, worked out the basic rules of inheritance through years of breeding experiments with garden pea plants (Pisum sativum), studying seven pairs of contrasting traits (e.g. tall vs. dwarf, round vs. wrinkled seeds).

Key vocabulary

P, F1, F2Parental generation; first filial (offspring) generation; second filial generation
Dominant / recessiveThe trait that appears in F1 is dominant; the one that re-appears in F2 is recessive
Genotype / phenotypeAn organism's genetic makeup vs. its observable trait
Homozygous / heterozygousTwo identical alleles (e.g. TT, tt) vs. two different alleles (Tt)

Monohybrid cross

Crosses a single trait. Mendel crossed pure-breeding tall (TT) with pure-breeding dwarf (tt) plants. All F1 offspring were tall and heterozygous (Tt). When F1 plants self-pollinated, the F2 generation showed tall and dwarf plants in a 3∶1 ratio.

Dihybrid cross

Tracks two traits at once (e.g. seed shape and seed color). Crossing two double-heterozygotes (RrYy × RrYy) produces a 9∶3∶3∶1 phenotypic ratio in F2 — 9 dominant-dominant : 3 dominant-recessive : 3 recessive-dominant : 1 recessive-recessive.

Test cross

Used to reveal an unknown genotype by crossing it with a known homozygous recessive individual.

  • If all offspring show the dominant trait → the unknown parent was homozygous dominant.
  • If offspring split ≈1∶1 dominant : recessive → the unknown parent was heterozygous.

Mendel's two laws

  • Law of segregation — the two alleles for a gene separate during meiosis, so each gamete carries only one allele. (Physical basis: Anaphase I of meiosis.)
  • Law of independent assortment — alleles of different genes sort into gametes independently of one another. (Physical basis: random alignment of chromosome pairs at Metaphase I.)
Exam angleRatios are the highest-yield numbers in this whole unit: 3∶1 (monohybrid F2) and 9∶3∶3∶1 (dihybrid F2). Be able to build a Punnett square from scratch, not just recall the ratio.
4.6

Sex determination

+

In humans, sex is determined by one pair of chromosomes: XX produces a female, XY produces a male. Every egg carries an X chromosome; sperm carry either an X or a Y. Because the father's sperm determines the combination, the father's gamete determines the sex of the child.

4.7

Non-Mendelian inheritance

+

Not every trait follows Mendel's simple dominant/recessive pattern. These four patterns are the main exceptions tested at this level.

Co-dominance

Both alleles are expressed fully and equally — neither masks the other. Example: the AB blood type, where both A and B antigens appear together on red blood cells.

Incomplete dominance

The heterozygote shows a blended phenotype in between the two homozygous forms — e.g. crossing red-flowered and white-flowered plants can produce pink-flowered offspring.

Multiple alleles — the ABO blood group

A single gene can have more than two alleles across a population, even though one individual only carries two. Human blood type is controlled by three alleles: IA, IB, and i. IA and IB are co-dominant with each other; both are dominant over i.

Rh factor

A separate blood-group gene from ABO. Rh+ is dominant over Rh−. Medically important during pregnancy: if an Rh− mother carries an Rh+ baby, her immune system can react against the baby's blood in later pregnancies, so Rh status is routinely checked.

Sex-linked inheritance

Genes carried on the X chromosome show a distinctive inheritance pattern because males (XY) only have one copy. A recessive X-linked allele is therefore expressed in any male who inherits it, while a female needs two copies (XhXh) to show the trait — which is why X-linked recessive conditions like hemophilia are far more common in males.

Environmental effects on phenotype

Genotype sets the potential, but environment can shape the final phenotype — temperature, nutrition, and other external factors can all influence how a trait is expressed.

4.8

Human pedigree analysis

+

A pedigree is a chart that traces a trait through a family tree across generations. By reading the pattern of who is and isn't affected, you can work out:

  • Whether the trait is dominant or recessive
  • Whether it's carried on an autosome or a sex chromosome
  • The likely genotypes of family members
  • The probability that future children will inherit it
4.9

Genetic disorders

+

A genetic disorder is a disease caused by a mutation — a change in the DNA sequence, which can arise from replication errors or environmental factors. Disorders fall into three categories:

1. Single-gene disorders

PatternHow it's expressedExample
Autosomal dominantShows up even when heterozygous; inherited the same regardless of parent's sexHuntington's disease
Autosomal recessiveNeeds two copies (one from each parent); carriers are unaffectedCystic fibrosis, albinism
X-linked recessiveFar more common in males (only need one copy); females need twoHemophilia

2. Chromosome disorders

Caused by changes in chromosome number or structure, usually from errors during cell division.

  • Aneuploidy — wrong chromosome number (e.g. 2n+1, 2n−1). Down syndrome (trisomy 21) is the best-known example: three copies of chromosome 21 instead of two.
  • Deletion — a chromosome segment is lost.
  • Inversion — a segment breaks off, flips, and reinserts backwards.
  • Translocation — a segment moves to a different chromosome.

3. Multifactorial disorders

Caused by a combination of multiple genes interacting with lifestyle/environmental factors (smoking, diet, sleep, pollution). Examples: diabetes and cancer.

4.10–4.11

Genetic testing, counseling & gene therapy

+

Genetic testing and counseling identify a person's or couple's risk of carrying or passing on a genetic disorder, helping them make informed reproductive and health decisions.

Gene therapy treats or prevents disease by introducing, removing, or correcting genetic material inside a patient's cells — directly targeting the faulty gene rather than just its symptoms.

4.12

Breeding

+

Breeding applies genetic principles to agriculture and animal husbandry to enhance desirable qualities. It can happen through selective breeding (artificial selection) or natural selection (no human intervention; better-adapted individuals survive and reproduce more).

Selective breeding

TypeDefinitionTrade-off
InbreedingMating genetically similar/related individuals to fix stable, "purebred" traitsIncreases the chance recessive (often harmful) traits appear
CrossbreedingMating unrelated individuals from different breeds or speciesBoosts desirable traits, but can dilute indigenous breeds

Plant breeding techniques

  • Mass selection — selecting many plants with the desired phenotype and pooling their seed; the simplest, oldest method.
  • Pure line selection — individually evaluating progeny from self-pollinated plants and releasing the best line.
  • Bulk method — growing F2 and later generations together in bulk before selecting individuals.
Ethiopian contextEthiopian farmers have long applied indigenous breeding knowledge — for example, selectively breeding long-tailed and long-horned sheep, and crossbreeding local Borena cattle with exotic sires to combine local resilience with improved productivity.
4.13

Bioinformatics — introduction

+

Bioinformatics is the use of computational tools and databases to collect, organize, and analyze large biological datasets — most notably DNA and protein sequences — making genome-scale genetics research possible.

Unit 4 — Self-test

1. Crossing TT × tt produces an F1 generation that is all:

Every offspring receives one T from the TT parent and one t from the tt parent → all Tt (heterozygous, tall).

2. What phenotypic ratio does a dihybrid F2 cross (RrYy × RrYy) produce?

Two independently assorting genes produce four phenotype classes in a 9∶3∶3∶1 ratio.

3. Why are X-linked recessive disorders like hemophilia more common in males?

Males are XY, so a single recessive allele on their one X is unmasked. Females (XX) need two copies to show the trait.

4. Down syndrome is an example of which category of genetic disorder?

Down syndrome is trisomy 21 — an extra copy of chromosome 21 — making it a chromosomal (aneuploidy) disorder.

5. Which process creates genetic variation through exchange of chromosome segments?

Crossing over swaps segments between homologous chromosomes during Prophase I, mixing parental gene combinations.

Musculoskeletal system

+

How a skeletal muscle contracts (sliding filament mechanism)

Each muscle fiber is packed with two protein filaments — thick myosin and thin actin — organized into repeating units called sarcomeres. A regulatory protein, troponin, controls when they can interact.

  1. Activation — a nerve impulse reaches the neuromuscular junction and triggers the sarcoplasmic reticulum to release calcium ions into the muscle cell.
  2. Contraction — calcium binds troponin, shifting tropomyosin out of the way so myosin can grip actin, forming cross-bridges. Using ATP as energy, myosin pulls the actin filaments inward, shortening the sarcomere.
  3. Recharging — ATP is resynthesized so myosin can keep cycling and maintain tension.
  4. Relaxation — once the nerve signal stops, calcium is pumped back into the sarcoplasmic reticulum, the actin–myosin link breaks, and the muscle relaxes.
IsometricMuscle generates tension without changing length
ConcentricMuscle shortens while contracting
EccentricMuscle lengthens while still under tension

The skeleton

The human skeleton has two divisions:

  • Axial skeleton — skull, vertebral column, ribcage: the body's central core.
  • Appendicular skeleton — limbs, shoulder girdle, pelvic girdle: everything attached to the core.

5.2

The reproductive system

+

The reproductive system enables sexual reproduction — producing gametes (sperm and eggs), and supporting fertilization and pregnancy.

Gametogenesis

The process of forming gametes through meiosis: spermatogenesis in males produces sperm; oogenesis in females produces eggs (ova).

The menstrual cycle

A monthly hormonal cycle controlled by a feedback loop between the brain and ovaries — rising and falling levels of FSH, LH, estrogen and progesterone regulate ovulation and prepare the uterine lining for a possible pregnancy.

Fertilization & pregnancy

Fertilization is the fusion of sperm and egg to form a zygote, which implants in the uterus and develops through pregnancy (gestation).

Contraceptive methods

Contraceptives intentionally prevent pregnancy using hormones, devices, or procedures. They fall into two broad mechanisms:

MechanismExamples
Barrier — physically blocks sperm from reaching the eggMale/female condom, diaphragm, cervical cap (condoms are also the only method that protects against STIs)
Non-barrier — hormonal or chemicalOral pills (combined & progesterone-only), IUD, implant, injection (Depo-Provera), emergency pill, vaginal ring, sterilization (tubal ligation / vasectomy)
MethodHow it worksNote
Oral contraceptive pillHormones suppress ovulation, thicken cervical mucus, thin uterine liningHighly effective when taken consistently

Infertility

Infertility can arise from causes in either partner — in women, factors include hormonal imbalance, blocked fallopian tubes, or age; in men, factors include low sperm count, hormonal problems, or urogenital infection. Lifestyle factors such as heavy drug or alcohol use can also contribute.

5.3

Harmful traditional practices

+

Practices that damage physical or mental health and violate a person's right to live free from harm, discrimination, and violence.

  • Early marriage — forcing underage girls into marriage; linked to school dropout, early pregnancy, and long-term health risk.
  • Abduction — kidnapping a girl to force marriage.
  • Gender-based violence — unwanted sexual contact, abuse, or harassment, including within marriage.
  • Female Genital Mutilation (FGM) — partial or total removal of external female genital organs.
Ethiopian contextFGM alone accounts for roughly 73% of recorded harmful traditional practices in Ethiopia, with about half of all such practices involving mutilation of skin or body parts.
5.4

Family planning

+

Family planning is the ability to anticipate and choose the number, timing, and spacing of children, considering a couple's financial, social, and emotional readiness.

Risks of not planning

  • Greater maternal health risk during pregnancy, birth, and the postpartum period.
  • Both very early and very late motherhood raise the risk of complications.
  • Untreated pregnancy/childbirth complications can lead to disability or death.

Family planning actions & services

Includes preventing unwanted pregnancy, STI protection and counseling, waiting at least 6 months after a miscarriage/abortion before trying again, and access to birth control or assisted reproductive technology.

5.5

Effects of alcohol, khat & other drugs

+

Substance use is closely linked to STI transmission and unwanted pregnancy because it impairs judgment and increases risk-taking.

  • Alcohol — lowers inhibition and judgment, increasing the likelihood of risky sexual behavior.
  • Khat (Catha edulis) — a shrub whose leaves are chewed as a stimulant; widely used across the Horn of Africa. It stimulates the central nervous system and is linked to insomnia, appetite loss, and increased risk-taking behavior.
  • Other drugs — marijuana (cannabis), cocaine, and heroin further impair decision-making and judgment, compounding the risks above.

Unit 5 — Self-test

1. Which muscle type is involuntary AND striated?

Cardiac muscle is unique: striated like skeletal muscle, but involuntary like smooth muscle.

2. What ion triggers the binding of actin and myosin during muscle contraction?

Calcium released from the sarcoplasmic reticulum binds troponin, exposing the myosin-binding sites on actin.

3. Which joint type allows movement in all directions, like the shoulder?

The ball-and-socket joint (shoulder, hip) is the only joint type that permits rotation in every direction.

4. Which contraceptive method is the only one that also protects against STIs?

Hormonal and device-based methods prevent pregnancy but don't block infection transmission — only condoms do both.
Unit Six

Population & natural resources

Zooming out from the individual to the population and the planet — how populations grow, how Ethiopia conserves its resources, and how human activity is reshaping the environment.

6.1

Population ecology

+

Size, density & dispersal

Population size is the total number of individuals; density is how many individuals occupy a given area; dispersal pattern describes how individuals are spread — clumped, uniform, or random.

Exponential vs. logistic growth

Exponential growthLogistic growth
ConditionsUnlimited resources, no limiting factorsResources are limited; growth slows as the population approaches capacity
Curve shapeJ-shapedS-shaped
Key termGrowth rate (r)Carrying capacity (K) — the maximum population an environment can sustain
Exponential growth climbs unchecked; logistic growth levels off at carrying capacity (K)

Demographic structure

A population's age and sex distribution can be visualized as a population pyramid — broad-based pyramids indicate a young, rapidly growing population, while more rectangular shapes indicate a stable or aging one. As countries develop, they tend to move through a demographic transition from high birth/death rates to low birth/death rates.

Population regulation

Factors that control population size fall into two categories:

  • Density-dependent — their effect intensifies as population density rises (e.g. competition for food, predation, disease spread).
  • Density-independent — affect the population regardless of its density (e.g. natural disasters, extreme weather).

6.3

Conservation of natural resources in Ethiopia

+

Conservation is the wise use, protection, and management of natural resources so they remain available for future generations — balancing current public benefit against long-term sustainability.

Ethiopia's varied topography and climate give it rich natural resources — wildlife, plants, water, and soil — but rapid population growth, industrialization, and urbanization are putting growing pressure on them.

Ethiopian wildlifeEthiopia hosts globally significant wildlife, including several endemic species — among them the Walia ibex, Stresemann's bushcrow, and the Ethiopian mountain chameleon — valued for ecological balance, genetic diversity, tourism, and the economy.

Conservation work in Ethiopia covers wildlife, plants, soil, and water — including soil and water conservation techniques (covered in more depth in section 6.6).

6.4

Impact of traffic accidents on wild & domestic animals

+

Road networks cutting through habitats lead to animal deaths from vehicle collisions and fragment habitats into smaller, more isolated patches — making it harder for wildlife and livestock populations to move, feed, and breed safely.

6.6

Indigenous conservation practices in Ethiopia

+

Indigenous knowledge is the body of understanding a community builds over generations about its local environment — in Ethiopia, this includes knowledge of astronomy, seasonal prediction, traditional medicine, and resource conservation, developed through long observation of the sun, moon, stars, animal behavior, and weather patterns.

The Konso people — UNESCO World HeritageThe soil and water conservation system developed by the Konso people of southwestern Ethiopia has been practiced for more than 400 years and is internationally recognized by UNESCO as a World Heritage site.

Konso conservation techniques

  • Terracing — dry-stone terraces built on hillsides that intercept runoff, reduce soil erosion, and create flat "saddles" for farming.
  • Contour ploughing — ploughing along the natural contour of a hill (rather than straight up/down) to slow water runoff.
  • Crop rotation — alternating different crops on the same land across seasons to maintain soil fertility.
  • Mixed cropping — growing multiple crop species together.
  • Surface mulching — covering soil with organic material to retain moisture and reduce erosion.
  • Agroforestry — integrating trees with crops or livestock on the same land.

Unit 6 — Self-test

1. What shape is a logistic growth curve?

Logistic growth slows as the population nears carrying capacity (K), producing a flattening S-curve — unlike the unchecked J-curve of exponential growth.

2. Which of these is a non-renewable natural resource?

Coal forms over millions of years — far slower than it's consumed — making it non-renewable, unlike forests and water which naturally replenish.

3. The Konso people's indigenous conservation system is internationally recognized by:

The Konso Cultural Landscape, including its terracing system, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

4. Build-up of pesticide residue at increasing levels up the food chain is called:

Bioaccumulation describes toxins concentrating further at each trophic level as one organism eats many of the level below.
